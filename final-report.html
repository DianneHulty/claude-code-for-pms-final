Release 4.2 · shipped 12 Aug 2026 · final report

Release 4.2 quietly benched four reliable responders.

We cut the time to answer a ping from 90 to 60 seconds. More pings ran out. Our scoring counts a missed ping exactly like a "no", and never lets it fade. Within three weeks, Meteor Mite, Farlight, The Undertow and Vesper went from 49 pings a week to 3, and nothing on anyone's screen said why.

Chapter 1 · What happened

One shorter clock. One old rule nobody had decided.

The villain here isn't a person. It's a default. Release 4.2 didn't add a new rule. It changed three numbers, and one of them switched on a rule that had been sitting in the code since 2019.

01

The clock got shorter

90s→60s

4.2 cut how long a ping stays on a responder's phone. Pings that people used to catch in time started running out. For the four, half their pings ran out in release week.

02

A miss counted as a "no"

take +0.08turn down −0.12miss −0.12time passing ±0

The routing code treats a missed ping exactly like a turn-down, and only a take earns points back. Nothing fades on its own. Wen asked in 2019 whether it should. Nobody answered.

03

Then the phone went quiet

6takes in a row, roughly, to recover

A lower score means you're asked later. A neighbour takes the callout about 61 seconds after your miss. You get fewer pings, so you can't earn points back. It's a trap: recovery takes 4 to 12 weeks, if it happens at all.

Why nobody saw it coming

The average looked fine

Headline acceptance recovered from 54% to 73%. Meanwhile, callouts nobody took roughly doubled. Four people sinking barely moves an average of sixteen.

It got called "the August dip"

The handoff said August is always soft. But the four's misses jumped first, and their pings fell after. A seasonal dip would cut pings first. And there's no data before 29 Jun to test the claim at all.

The quietest handlers didn't file

Kip, Aunt Dot, Halloran and Mr. Ambrose file almost nothing, and never did. The support queue undercounted the problem, so it only showed up in interviews and the data.

Chapter 3 · The fix

Back in Rotation: a miss is not a no.

Helen asked for what we'd build instead of quietly changing a number: something Kip would notice, and something a quiet responder would feel differently about. These are the four changes, each one something a person notices.

Phase 1

A miss is not a no

When a ping runs out, Mite's phone says the callout went to someone else, and that a miss doesn't count the way turning one down does.

Who notices: the responder
Phase 1

A bad stretch fades

A low score drifts back toward normal over time, so one bad week doesn't follow a responder forever. This answers Wen's 2019 question.

Who notices: both
Phase 2

A check-in, not silence

After 3 misses in a row, Mite's phone asks "Still on tonight?" Yes puts Mite back in line. No answer marks Mite as away, which Kip can see.

Who notices: the responder. Also covers Availability Confidence, which was due in 4.2.
Phase 2

The card says why

Kip's console flags when a responder is being asked much less than usual, gives the reason, and shows who's covering.

Who notices: the handler. Needs scores to be saved, which they aren't today.
The trade-off: each miss costs about 60 seconds before the callout moves on. If misses stop moving people down the line, someone who keeps missing stays first and slows down real incidents. The check-in prevents that, so it's part of the fix, not an extra. And both scoring changes ship together: in my Module 4 replay, fading alone barely helped, and a one-time reset only held if the miss penalty also changed.
